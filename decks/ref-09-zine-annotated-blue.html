02northbeam — project proposal

about us

the studio, kreuzberg — 2044

northbeam is a studio of eleven people working on brand systems for companies that ship software. we were founded in 2038 by two designers who were tired of handing over files that nobody could maintain, and we have kept the same rule since: whatever we draw, someone else has to be able to run.

that rule decides everything else. it is why our deliverable is never a folder of images, why we write down the reasoning next to the artwork, and why we measure what we make instead of arguing about it.

we work in short blocks with the people who will own the result. no long silences, no reveal at the end. you see the work while it is still cheap to change, and we spend the last week of every engagement teaching rather than drawing.

every engagement ends with a half-day where we hand over the reasoning, not only the files: what each decision was for, what we would change first, and which parts are safe to break.

the studio is deliberately small. eleven people is the largest number that can sit around one table and still hear each other, and everything about the way we work assumes that table exists.

we take four projects a year. it is the number that lets the same two people stay on a project from the first conversation to the last, which is the only way we know to keep a promise made in week one.

03northbeam — about us

the problem

the identity works when you draw it and fails when anyone else does. four teams ship four versions of the same button, the palette drifts one hue per quarter, and nobody can say which file is current. none of that is a drawing problem: every one of those decisions was made by someone who could not find the answer in under a minute.

a system nobody can run is a system that only exists on the day it is presented.

04northbeam — the problem

our approach

three rules, and they are the same three on every project we take. they are not a method: they are what we do instead of one, and each of them exists because of a project where its absence cost us a month.

01

short blocks

we work in blocks of two weeks, and each block ends with something you can use rather than something you can look at. a component, a token set, a page that runs in your own build — never a board of proposals. the block is short because that is the interval at which a wrong assumption is still cheap to unmake: at six weeks the drawing has already been built on, and the argument stops being about the drawing. nothing is held back to be shown later, and there is no phase in which we go quiet. we would rather be wrong in week two than right in week eight, and the calendar is written to keep it cheap.

02

owners in the room

the people who will maintain the result are in the room from the first block, not shown the result in the last one. that is a scheduling cost for you and it is the single thing that decides whether the system survives us. a design handed to a team that did not argue with it is a design that team will route around within two quarters — politely, one exception at a time, until the exceptions are the system and the original file is something nobody opens. the room is small on purpose: four people who can decide, and nobody there to report back. if the person who will own the result cannot come, we move the block.

03

measured, not defended

every rule we write down comes with the measurement that decides whether it holds — contrast on the real background, the character count of a justified column, the smallest body on the page. it means we lose arguments to numbers, including our own, and it means you can check the system without us. a rule that cannot be measured is a preference, and a preference does not belong in a document a team has to run for two years. we lost the argument about this very column twice — once to the character count, once to the contrast of its grey — and the document is better for both losses.

05northbeam — our approach

the process

four stages, eleven weeks, and a signature at the end of each. nothing starts before the previous stage is signed, and nothing is redone for free after it is.

the token set ships before the visual language is signed off — it is the only order that survives a change of mind.

  1. 01

    audit

    every surface you ship, side by side, with the decision that made each one differ. you sign off the list of what is broken before we draw anything.

    weeks 1–2
  2. 02

    language

    type scale, palette, spacing and the measurements that keep them honest. delivered as tokens first, drawings second.

    weeks 3–5
  3. 03

    build

    the components your teams actually use, built in your own stack with your engineers, not handed over as a folder of images.

    weeks 6–9
  4. 04

    handover

    a half-day on the reasoning: what each decision was for, what we would change first, and which parts are safe to break.

    weeks 10–11

case study

meridian rail came to us with the same problem, one size larger: nine product teams, four of them in other companies, and a wayfinding system that had been redrawn three times in five years without ever being written down.

we ran the four stages once, with their engineers in the room from the first week, and then we left. the thing we are proudest of is not in the drawings: they have shipped eleven new surfaces since, and we drew none of them.

the system is still the one we handed over, and it is still being argued with — the change log has two hundred entries and every one of them names the measurement it was decided on.
